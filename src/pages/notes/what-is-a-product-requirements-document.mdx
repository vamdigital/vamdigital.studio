A Product Requirements Document, usually shortened to PRD, describes the product or feature a team intends to build.

It brings the problem, users, expected behaviour, scope and measures of success into one place. This gives product, design, engineering and QA a shared understanding before implementation begins.

A PRD does not need to provide every technical detail. Its main purpose is to explain what needs to be built and why it matters.

## What does a PRD define?

A PRD describes the expected features, functionality and constraints of a product or feature.

It should help the team answer questions such as:

- What problem are we solving?
- Who are we solving it for?
- What should the product allow them to do?
- What behaviour is required?
- What is included in the initial scope?
- What is deliberately excluded?
- How will we know whether the result is successful?

The document should contain enough detail for the team to make informed decisions without prescribing every aspect of the implementation.

## What does a PRD contain?

The exact structure varies between teams and projects, but a useful PRD commonly includes the following sections.

### Product overview

The overview gives the reader a concise explanation of the proposed product or feature.

It should describe:

- The problem being addressed
- The people affected by the problem
- The proposed outcome
- Why the work matters

Someone unfamiliar with the project should be able to read this section and understand its purpose.

### Goals and non-goals

Goals define what the work is expected to achieve.

Non-goals are equally useful because they explain what the team is not attempting to solve in the current piece of work.

Making both explicit helps prevent assumptions from quietly expanding the scope.

### User stories or use cases

User stories describe the requirement from the user's perspective.

A common format is:

> As a user, I want to perform an action so that I can achieve an outcome.

For example:

> As a returning customer, I want to view my previous orders so that I can find something I purchased earlier.

Use cases can provide more detail by describing the steps a person takes, the system's expected response and any alternative or failure paths.

### Functional requirements

Functional requirements describe what the product must do.

For an order-history feature, they might include requirements such as:

- Display the customer's previous orders
- Sort orders with the most recent first
- Allow the customer to open an individual order
- Show an appropriate empty state when no orders exist

These requirements describe observable behaviour without necessarily deciding how the engineering team must implement it.

### Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements describe qualities and constraints surrounding the feature.

These can include:

- Accessibility expectations
- Performance requirements
- Browser or device support
- Security and privacy constraints
- Reliability expectations
- Localisation requirements

A feature can satisfy its functional requirements and still provide a poor experience if these wider qualities are ignored.

### Assumptions and constraints

Assumptions record things the team currently believes to be true.

Constraints describe boundaries within which the solution must operate, such as an existing API, a supported browser range or a delivery dependency.

Writing these down makes uncertainty visible. It also gives the team something specific to revisit when new information appears.

### Success metrics

Success metrics explain how the team will evaluate the outcome.

The appropriate measures depend on the feature. They could relate to task completion, adoption, reliability, performance or another observable result.

Metrics should connect to the original problem rather than simply measuring whether the feature was delivered.

## Why is a PRD important?

A PRD creates a shared reference for the people involved in delivering a product.

### It creates clarity

Product, design, engineering and QA can interpret the same idea differently. A written description exposes those differences early, while they are still easier to discuss.

### It supports planning

Clear requirements help the team identify dependencies, estimate effort, divide the work and decide what should be prioritised.

An estimate may still change as the team learns more, but it begins from a better-defined understanding of the work.

### It provides a reference point

During design, development and testing, the team can return to the PRD when questions arise.

This does not mean that the document replaces conversation. It gives those conversations a shared starting point and records the decisions that follow.

### It helps manage scope

A PRD makes the agreed scope visible. When a new request appears, the team can decide whether it belongs in the current work, should replace something else or should be considered separately.

The aim is not to freeze every requirement permanently. It is to make changes deliberate rather than accidental.

### It improves cross-functional communication

A PRD is particularly valuable when work moves between different roles, teams or locations.

It helps everyone understand the same problem and expected outcome without depending entirely on information passed through meetings or private conversations.

## When is a PRD used?

A PRD remains useful throughout the product-development lifecycle.

### Before development

The document is normally created during discovery or planning, once the problem is understood well enough to consider a solution.

At this stage, it helps the team:

- Agree on the problem
- Define the intended outcome
- Explore the scope
- Identify unanswered questions
- Decide whether the work is ready to begin

The document does not need to be perfect before anyone can discuss it. Writing it often reveals the areas that still need investigation.

### During design and development

The PRD becomes a reference while designers explore the interaction and engineers decide how to implement it.

New information may require a requirement to be clarified or changed. When that happens, the PRD should be updated so that it continues to reflect the team's current agreement.

This makes it a living document rather than a snapshot that becomes outdated as soon as development starts.

### During testing

QA and engineering can use the requirements to identify expected behaviour, important scenarios and failure cases.

The PRD does not replace detailed test plans or acceptance criteria, but it provides the product context behind them.

### After launch

After release, the team can compare the delivered outcome with the original goals and success measures.

This helps answer two separate questions:

- Did we build what we intended to build?
- Did it solve the problem we intended to solve?

Delivering every listed requirement does not automatically mean the product was successful. The outcome still needs to be evaluated.

## A lightweight PRD structure

A PRD does not have to become a large document. A smaller feature may only need a concise structure:

```text
Title

Problem
What problem are we trying to solve?

Users
Who experiences this problem?

Goals
What outcome should this work achieve?

Non-goals
What is outside the current scope?

User stories
What should users be able to do?

Requirements
How should the feature behave?

Constraints and assumptions
What boundaries or uncertainties should the team know about?

Success measures
How will we evaluate the result?

Open questions
What still needs to be decided?
```

The amount of detail should be proportional to the size, risk and uncertainty of the work.

## A shared understanding, not just a document

The value of a PRD does not come from completing a template. It comes from the conversations and decisions that the document captures.

A useful PRD gives the team a shared understanding of the problem, the intended outcome and the boundaries of the work. It provides direction without trying to replace design exploration, technical planning or ongoing collaboration.

When treated as a living reference, it helps a team move from an idea to implementation with fewer assumptions and clearer decisions.
